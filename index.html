Ramp Autonomy: a ROS 2 / Gazebo stack for ramp ascent and descent on a loaded AMR
from ROS Discourse General

Hi all,

I’ve open-sourced a project I’ve been building for a while, and I’d like feedback
from people who know Nav2 better than I do.

github.com/skunal3318/Ramp-Autonomy. Apache-2.0, ROS 2 Jazzy, Gazebo Harmonic (gz-sim 8), Nav2 MPPI.

The problem

Terrain-aware navigation is a real field, but the default Nav2 tuning path, MPPI
profiles included, still assumes flat ground, and that is what most people start
from. Construction-site AMRs moving material between floors cross temporary ramps
constantly. On a grade, three things a flat-floor stack treats as constants
become variables at once:

  • IMU pitch bias corrupting odometry exactly when you need it
  • a payload shifting the effective CG, so the tip-over margin changes in real time
  • wheel slip as a distinct failure mode with no flat-ground equivalent

So the ramp gets treated as its own control and safety problem rather than a path
to power through.

What’s in it

A five-state FSM (APPROACH → ASCENT → TRANSITION → DESCENT → EXIT, with HALT
pre-emptable from any state) driven end-to-end through real FollowPath/MPPI
control. A LiDAR slope and break-point classifier feeding a look-ahead transition
trigger. A safety layer covering tip-over margin, wheel slip and motion stall, where
any one signal can veto the controller. Two custom nav2_mppi_controller critics.

Two things that might be useful to others here

1. Ascent and descent ended up with opposite MPPI profiles. Ascent runs a
tight heading tolerance (5°) and low angular authority (0.4 rad/s), and measures
driftless at those numbers. Descent runs both loosened (20°, 0.6 rad/s) so it has
the steering authority to act on cross-track error that ascent never accumulates.
Same ramp, inverted profile. Worth saying that a good chunk of the descent drift
I originally tuned against turned out to be my own test harness turning the robot
in the wrong place, so treat the asymmetry as what this stack settled on rather
than a law of ramps.

2. Capping speed to control slip on a descent made things dramatically worse.
My first braking version capped vx_max. On the same descent leg:

approach time max slip HALTs
no braking 17.01 s 1.000 0
cap vx_max 76.44 s 11.148 146
cap ax_max/az_max 17.77 s 1.000 0

Capping the speed ceiling forces the controller to decelerate harder to comply,
and on a slope that larger acceleration transient is what causes slip in the first
place. The brake read “still slipping”, cut speed further, demanded a harder
correction, and spiralled. Slip on a grade tracks acceleration transients, not
cruising speed.

Worth being precise about the third row, because it is easy to read as a fix it
has not earned: slip never crossed the brake’s threshold on that run, so the
acceleration-capping version is demonstrated harmless, not demonstrated
effective. Its intervention branch has never actually been exercised. Provoking a
mid-descent slip spike to test it properly is still on the list.

There’s also a PathAlignCritic gotcha worth knowing: its score() returns
immediately unless the robot has advanced offset_from_furthest points into the
path. The default is 20; my descent paths have ~15 points total, so the critic
was structurally disabled for the entire descent leg across three tuning
attempts that went nowhere. That one cost me a lot of time.

Status, honestly

Simulation only. Sim-to-real is an explicit separate phase rather than
something I’ve quietly deferred. The trusted 7-cell matrix is 1 PASS, 5 MARGINAL, 1 FAIL, and
there’s no planner_server/bt_navigator/map yet; every test drives the robot
with a hand-built FollowPath goal. All of that is written up rather than hidden.

Everything is verified against Gazebo ground truth rather than against the FSM’s
own report of its state. docs/retrospective.md has two cases where that
distinction mattered enormously, including a controller that tracked its plan
correctly and still drove off a ledge because localisation was wrong.

What I’d value

  • Anyone who knows MPPI well telling me where the parameter profiles are wrong.
    They’re honest placeholders that happen to work, not a tuned result.
  • Reproduction. If you re-run a published number and disagree with me, that’s
    more useful than agreement, and there’s an issue template for it.
  • Anyone who has run a real AMR on a ramp with a shifting payload. I’d like to
    know where the simulation is lying to me.
  • Mechanical review, which I did not expect to need at all. Someone worked the
    traction balance on the grade today, and following it through the load transfer
    that unloads the uphill axle under braking, my descent acceleration ceiling of
    2.0 m/s^2 may be roughly three times what those wheels can actually take before
    they slip. On this chassis CG height over wheelbase is 0.49, which is tall
    enough that the transfer term dominates. It is paper maths until I measure it in
    the simulator, and it is issue 25.

Twenty issues are open at a range of sizes. Being straight about the easy end:
the tasks needing no simulator all got taken today, so what is currently unclaimed
needs either ROS or a working Gazebo install. There is also a standing request for
anyone with a real robot and a ramp, and docs/hardware-test-plan.md now sets out
what a first session would actually involve rather than leaving it vague.

Four pull requests merged today, which I mention only because “will my PR sit
there for a month” is a fair thing to wonder about an unknown repo.

Happy to answer anything here.

Kunal

MOLA cloud: free online LiDAR SLAM from ROS bags, plus camera and LiDAR-camera calibration
from ROS Discourse General

Hi ROS friends!

This is an announcement that we have just opened MOLA cloud (https://app.mola-slam.org), a free web service that runs MOLA, our open-source LiDAR odometry and SLAM framework for ROS 2, on your own data files: you upload a ROS 2 bag and download a 3D map.

Why

The “Traditional Good-old Way” ™ to get a map out of a bag is to install the packages, find the right topics and frames, adapt a launch file, run it and wait. That is still the way to go inside live robots, and probably most of you can do it (with or without help of your fav AI agent!), but this MOLA cloud service will run the same open-source packages you can install with apt, without wasting your time. Often one just wants to:

  • check that a recording is good before processing it for real,
  • get a quick map of a dataset you have just downloaded,
  • share a map with someone who has no ROS machine at hand,
  • or get fancy maps for use in teaching classes or as student practices.

For those cases, MOLA cloud does it from the browser.

From a ROS 2 bag to a map

  • Input: a ROS 2 bag, .mcap or a zipped .db3 folder (ROS 1 .bag files also work). Upload it from the browser or import it by URL from S3, Zenodo, Hugging Face or Google Cloud Storage.
  • Topics and frames: the app reads the bag and proposes the LiDAR (sensor_msgs/PointCloud2), IMU, GNSS (sensor_msgs/NavSatFix) and wheel odometry (nav_msgs/Odometry) topics, and the vehicle frame from /tf. You can also customize any of them if autodetection doesn’t work.
  • Method: LiDAR-inertial SLAM with loop closures (mola_mapper on top of MOLA’s LiDAR-inertial odometry), or LiDAR-only odometry for bags without an IMU.
  • What you download:
    • the point cloud map as LAS and PLY, for CloudCompare, QGIS and other tools;
    • the MOLA metric map (.mm) and keyframe map (.simplemap), to reuse with mm-viewer, sm2mm or MOLA’s localization mode;
    • the trajectory in TUM format, both loop-closed and odometry-only;
    • previews, statistics and the job log, which lists the exact package versions used.
  • Geo-referencing: if the bag has GNSS fixes, the map comes geo-referenced: a LAS file in WGS-84, a KMZ for Google Earth and the trajectory in WGS-84. RTK receivers are recognized and weighted as such.
  • 3D viewer: results can also be inspected in the browser (on phones too), colored by height or intensity, with the trajectory and, for geo-referenced maps, an aerial view over orthophotos.

This is an example run from a mobile robot at our University campus:

Camera intrinsics, for free

The same app calibrates camera intrinsics:

  • Input: a video (MP4, MOV, MKV or WebM, for example from your phone), a ZIP of images, or a ROS 2 bag with an image topic.
  • Patterns (or none!): checkerboard, ChArUco, symmetric and asymmetric circle grids, and Kalibr’s AprilGrid. The app detects which pattern is in the recording; you can also find intrinsics from a random video, without any pattern. Pure Bundle Adjustment in the wild
  • Lens models: pinhole (plumb_bob) and fisheye (equidistant).
  • Output: a camera_info.yaml ready for your driver’s camera_info_url, OpenCV and MRPT YAML files, the RMS reprojection error and a coverage plot.

On the EuRoC calibration sequences, the focal length comes out about 0.5 % from the official calibration, with an RMS error of 0.17 px on the AprilGrid sequence.

## LiDAR-camera calibration, also free

MOLA cloud also finds the extrinsic calibration between a LiDAR and a camera, that is, where the camera sits relative to the LiDAR. The frames where the board is still and visible to both sensors are selected automatically, so there is no frame-by-frame review to do.

Free, with limits

MOLA cloud is free. To keep it sustainable and to avoid abuse, each account has a monthly usage limit. Jobs run on our server in Spain (EU), in isolated containers, and taking care of data privacy.

Links

  • App: https://app.mola-slam.org
  • ROS bag to map, how it works: ROS bag to 3D point cloud map, online - MOLA documentation
  • Camera calibration guide: Camera calibration online, from a video - MOLA documentation
  • Prefer to run it locally? Build a map - MOLA documentation
  • Source code: GitHub - MOLAorg/mola: A Modular Optimization framework for Localization and mApping (MOLA) · GitHub

It is a new service, so feedback is very welcome! Tell us how it worked, in this thread, as an issue at Issues · MOLAorg/mola · GitHub, or at support@mola-slam.org. We would especially like to hear which LiDARs and bags work for you and which do not, to keep improving.

ROS 2 Jazzy for macOS (Apple Silicon): release 26.11.20.9, self-contained, Gazebo included, Nav2 up in 78 s
from ROS Discourse General

A new ROS 2 Jazzy for Apple Silicon Macs is out on Homebrew: ros2-jazzy 26.11.20.9. It’s built natively on macOS from source, with no VM or Docker. Installation and setup are in the tap: idesign0/homebrew-ros2.

Video (real speed): a TurtleBot 4 launches with Nav2, undocks and drives 11.6 m across the warehouse world on an M2.

ROS 2 Jazzy on macOS (Apple Silicon): TurtleBot 4 + Nav2 in Gazebo, real speed

TL;DR

  • About 1,870 packages from 638 source repos. Most of the ~200 not shipped are Linux-only (kernel or device APIs, CUDA) or depend on one.
  • Self-contained: the Homebrew libraries it was built against ship inside the package, so a Homebrew update on your Mac can’t break it any more.
  • Gazebo Harmonic included (Ogre 2 on Metal). setup.zsh sets every path, so remove any old GZ_* / QML2_IMPORT_PATH exports.
  • Faster startup: libraries are dead-stripped. A TurtleBot 4 + Nav2 sim (Gazebo GUI + RViz) went from 2–3 min to 78 s.

What changed compared to the previous Jazzy

Before 26.11.20.9
Homebrew dependencies linked against your Homebrew, relinked in post_install (broke on abseil/DART updates) bundled in the keg: 215 libraries, 28 frameworks; nothing loads from Homebrew at run time
Gazebo separate copy, GZ_* exports in your shell Harmonic from inside the keg (opt/gz_*_vendor), no exports
Direct library links ~190 per Nav2 server 6 per Nav2 server, 77 % fewer across the tree (-dead_strip_dylibs)
TurtleBot 4 + Nav2 bring-up 2–3 min robot + map ~22 s, full stack 78 s
ros2 CLI ~0.4 s per command

Also included: MoveIt 2, Nav2, ros2_control, Autoware Core, MOLA, RTAB-Map, Cartographer, PCL, Webots, MuJoCo, Open-RMF and MAVROS, plus Zenoh, Cyclone DDS and iceoryx as middleware.

Tip for big launches: Cyclone DDS on loopback

With dozens of nodes (Nav2, TurtleBot), this profile was the most reliable one on macOS:

export RMW_IMPLEMENTATION=rmw_cyclonedds_cpp
export CYCLONEDDS_URI='<CycloneDDS><Domain><General><Interfaces><NetworkInterface name="lo0" multicast="true"/></Interfaces></General><Discovery><ParticipantIndex>none</ParticipantIndex></Discovery></Domain></CycloneDDS>'
  • ParticipantIndex none: the default per-process index pool runs out with big launches.
  • lo0: discovery multicast over Wi-Fi was lossy. The same launch came up in 20 s, several minutes or not at all; on loopback it comes up in ~22 s every run. This only works when all nodes are on one Mac.
  • Don’t combine it with ROS_AUTOMATIC_DISCOVERY_RANGE=LOCALHOST.

The Fast DDS shared-memory issue from my RMW measurements post is fixed in the next build. That build carries eProsima/Fast-DDS#6632 (still in review upstream) in our Fast DDS fork.

Known issues

  • Nav2 map_server can’t read .pgm maps yet: the bundled GraphicsMagick is missing its decoders. Until the next build: export DYLD_LIBRARY_PATH=$(brew --prefix graphicsmagick)/lib:$DYLD_LIBRARY_PATH.
  • Building your own workspace against it works for about 96 % of packages (find_package() from a clean overlay). Some gz components and Boost.Python still fail; that’s what I’m working on now.

Upstream first

I don’t want a macOS fork of ROS 2. 101 of the 638 repos are forks carrying macOS fixes, each tracking its upstream branch, so upstream changes land automatically. The fixes go back upstream: so far 76 PRs in 44 projects, 40 merged (Nav2, MoveIt 2, ros2_control, RViz, rosbag2, MOLA, …).

Build system: idesign0/ros2_macOS. If something doesn’t work on your Mac, please open an issue in the tap. Reports from other Macs and macOS versions are especially welcome.

Fault correlation for ROS 2: one cause, many faults (and what automotive does about it)
from ROS Discourse General

I have dug through noisy logs many times just to find the one real cause. Two situations keep coming back.

At startup, every robot I have worked with throws a pile of errors because drivers are still connecting and nodes are waiting for each other. Most go away on their own, but they sit in the log next to the one that matters.

Then there are chain reactions. One thing breaks and a minute later other nodes report missing data, timeouts and a failed bringup. I already know this is really one fault, but the robot reports it as many.

The case we rebuilt

We staged a startup with Nav2 Jazzy and AMCL in which a Hokuyo Ethernet lidar never connects, as with a pulled data cable or a wrong subnet. The texts are verbatim from urg_node and Nav2, replayed at their real repeat rates.

urg_node reports the diagnostic status “Not Connected”. AMCL publishes map to odom only after it processes a scan, so it never publishes it here. It logs nothing about the missing scans.

global_costmap logs “Timed out waiting for transform from base_link to map to become available” every half second at INFO and fails to activate after 60 seconds. lifecycle_manager_navigation reports “Failed to bring up all requested nodes. Aborting bringup.” and bt_navigator rejects the goal.

The launch log ends up with 164 messages on 222 lines. The lidar error is in there 29 times, between 120 costmap messages. Nothing in the log links one to the other.

How AUTOSAR handles this

Cars have had this for years. The AUTOSAR Classic Diagnostic Event Manager has event dependencies (SWS DEM R24-11, section 7.3.6). Events are assigned to components with parent relations and priorities. A FAILED report while a parent component is FAILED is a “consecutive fault” and is not stored in the failure memory. A causality delay time lets a failure be reclassified when a parent or higher priority failure is reported shortly after it.

The same idea in ros2_medkit

For startup faults, when healing is enabled, PASSED reports heal a fault and take it off the active list.

For the chain reaction we wrote one rule. It names the lidar fault as the cause, the scan, costmap, lifecycle and goal rejection codes as its symptoms, and a time window:

correlation:
  enabled: true
  default_window_ms: 500
  patterns:
    scan_dependent_localization:
      codes: ["LIDAR_SCAN_TOPIC_NO_EVENTS"]
    nav2_global_bringup:
      codes: ["GLOBAL_COSTMAP_*", "NAV_LIFECYCLE_*", "BT_NAVIGATOR_GOAL_REJECTED"]
  rules:
    - id: lidar_not_connected_blocks_localization
      name: "Lidar driver is not connected"
      mode: hierarchical
      window_ms: 5000
      root_cause:
        codes: ["LIDAR_NOT_CONNECTED"]
      symptoms:
        - pattern: scan_dependent_localization
        - pattern: nav2_global_bringup
      mute_symptoms: true
      auto_clear_with_root: true

A symptom that arrives after a report of the cause and inside the window is linked to it and muted. The window is measured from the latest report of the cause. That matters here because the costmap gives up a full minute after the lidar first fails. The rule works on fault codes that nodes report to the fault manager, so the reporters decide what a code means.

With this rule, GET /api/v1/faults returned one fault, LIDAR_NOT_CONNECTED, with muted_count 6. ?include_muted=true lists all seven with a root_cause_code for each symptom. Unlike AUTOSAR’s failure memory, a muted symptom stays stored.

Suggesting rules from fault history

After we first shared this, someone asked whether the rules have to stay hand-written (related comment). The fault history already holds what is needed to suggest them: which codes always follow which and how long after.

We would like the system to propose a rule once a pattern holds across many bringups, with an engineer approving it. A wrong rule would mute a real fault. The same history could also flag a symptom that shows up without its usual cause, which often means something changed on the robot. None of this exists yet.

Repo: GitHub - selfpatch/ros2_medkit: ros2_medkit - diagnostics gateway for ROS 2 robots. Faults, live data, operations, scripts, locking, triggers, and OTA updates via REST API. No SSH, no custom tooling. · GitHub

API responses and how to run the example: One Cause, Many Faults: Fault Correlation in ROS 2 | selfpatch.ai

Two questions for the packages you maintain:

  • Which failures report their symptoms before the underlying cause, and how do you handle that ordering today?
  • And would you trust a rule that was suggested from history, if you had to approve it first?

ROS 2 on macOS: measured topic rates for each RMW, a Fast DDS shared-memory pitfall, and tuned presets
from ROS Discourse General

I run ROS 2 Jazzy and Kilted natively on macOS (Apple Silicon) through my Homebrew tap, idesign0/homebrew-ros2. In my thesis setup, a 2 kHz laser height sensor (std_msgs/Float64) arrived at only a few hundred Hz on the Mac, while the same setup gave the full 2 kHz on Linux.

I benchmarked every RMW on the Mac to find out why, how fast topics can go, and which settings get the most out of each option. Everything below can be reproduced with GitHub - idesign0/ros2-macos-rmw-bench: How fast can ROS 2 nodes talk on macOS? Inter-process rate/latency benchmark for Fast DDS, Cyclone DDS (+iceoryx), Zenoh and composition, with reproducers for macOS-specific issues. · GitHub.

TL;DR

  • Fast DDS’s default shared-memory transport (SHM) has a ~5.7 ms polling tick on macOS. Messages between processes arrive in bursts with ~3 ms median latency, at any rate. A KEEP_LAST(1) subscriber at 2 kHz receives only ~350–450 Hz, and under heavy load delivery collapses to ~0 %. Linux is not affected. Root cause and proposed fix: [macOS] SHM transport wakes readers on a ~5.7 ms polling tick: Boost.Interprocess falls back to `spin_semaphore` on Apple · Issue #6639 · eProsima/Fast-DDS · GitHub.

  • Easiest fix: export RMW_IMPLEMENTATION=rmw_cyclonedds_cpp. Full 2 kHz at ~90 µs, clean to 50 kHz.

  • Best tuned options:

    Need Option Result
    Small messages, fast, no extra daemon Zenoh with batching off lossless to ~90 kHz at ~100 µs p99 (needs Zenoh’s router)
    Large messages Zenoh + SHM 4 MB at 1961 Hz (~7.8 GB/s), 0.3 ms
    Absolute fastest (C++ only) Cyclone + iceoryx 200 kHz at 10 µs p50 / 27 µs p99
    Staying on Fast DDS FASTDDS_BUILTIN_TRANSPORTS=UDPv4 clean to 20 kHz. Ignored with ROS_AUTOMATIC_DISCOVERY_RANGE=LOCALHOST

Setup

  • Hardware and OS: MacBook Pro 13" M2 (Mac14,7, 8 cores), macOS 27.0.1, Apple clang.
  • ROS 2: Kilted (Fast DDS 3.2.5, Cyclone DDS 0.10.5 + iceoryx 2.0.8, rmw_zenoh) and Jazzy (Fast DDS 2.14.7), from the tap above.
  • Benchmark: C++ (rclcpp) publisher and subscriber in two separate processes, built in Release. Each message carries its send time; the subscriber measures received rate, loss, duplicates and one-way latency.
  • “Clean” means at least 99.9 % of messages received and p99 latency under 1 ms.

1. A 2 kHz Float64 sensor stream (subscriber KEEP_LAST(1), best effort, rclcpp::spin)

RMW / config Received of 2000 Hz Latency p50 Latency p99
Fast DDS, default (SHM + UDP) 354–402 Hz 0.5–1.0 ms 5.2–9.8 ms
Fast DDS, default, depth 100 2000 Hz 2.9 ms 5.6–6.3 ms
Fast DDS, FASTDDS_BUILTIN_TRANSPORTS=UDPv4 1995 Hz 82–87 µs 172–204 µs
Cyclone DDS 1998–2000 Hz 85–97 µs 151–163 µs
Cyclone DDS + iceoryx 1998–2000 Hz 33–36 µs 69–71 µs
Zenoh 1981–1994 Hz 127–149 µs 271–275 µs
Zenoh, batching off 1988–2000 Hz 150 µs 190 µs

On Fast DDS the rate is fine with a deep queue, but every message is ~3 ms late: samples come in bursts every ~5.7 ms, and a KEEP_LAST(1) subscriber keeps only the last one of each burst.

2. How fast can you go? (Float64, two processes, subscriber depth 100)

RMW / config Clean up to p50 / p99 there Above that
Cyclone + iceoryx (C++ only, see caveats) 200 kHz 10 / 27 µs 300 kHz: 98.8 %

4. Why Fast DDS SHM behaves like this on macOS

Fast DDS waits for SHM data on an interprocess semaphore from its bundled Boost.Interprocess. On macOS, Boost disables the native process-shared primitives (Darwin’s pthread_cond is not truly process-shared, and sem_init returns ENOSYS), so it falls back to a spin semaphore. After a short spin, that semaphore sleeps for half a system tick: sysconf(_SC_CLK_TCK) = 100, so nanosleep(5 ms), ≈ 6 ms in practice.

Shortening that one sleep (an injected nanosleep shim) made the problem disappear, which confirms the cause, but at a CPU cost. A proper fix with macOS 14.4’s os_sync_wait_on_address woke a second process in 6–10 µs in my prototype. Full analysis: [macOS] SHM transport wakes readers on a ~5.7 ms polling tick: Boost.Interprocess falls back to `spin_semaphore` on Apple · Issue #6639 · eProsima/Fast-DDS · GitHub.

Presets: best settings for each option

A. Cyclone DDS: simplest, recommended default

export RMW_IMPLEMENTATION=rmw_cyclonedds_cpp
  • No daemon. 2 kHz at ~90 µs; clean to 50 kHz, lossless to ~150 kHz with a growing p99.
  • Tuning: socket buffers, MaxMessageSize and FragmentSize made no meaningful difference. The defaults are already its best.
  • Gotcha: if your CYCLONEDDS_URI sets <ParticipantIndex>none</ParticipantIndex> (a common tip for many nodes), don’t also set ROS_AUTOMATIC_DISCOVERY_RANGE=LOCALHOST. In my test that combination found no peers.
  • Avoid for very large messages: 4 MB arrived at only 16 Hz. Use Zenoh + SHM or iceoryx for those.

B. Zenoh: tuned, best all-rounder for high rates

ros2 run rmw_zenoh_cpp rmw_zenohd &        # router, once per machine
export RMW_IMPLEMENTATION=rmw_zenoh_cpp
export ZENOH_CONFIG_OVERRIDE='transport/link/tx/queue/batching/enabled=false'
# large messages: also enable shared memory (only used for messages > 512 B)
export ZENOH_CONFIG_OVERRIDE='transport/link/tx/queue/batching/enabled=false;transport/shared_memory/enabled=true'
  • Batching off is the important knob. At 50 kHz it cuts p99 from 2.1 ms to 65–112 µs. With it, Zenoh is lossless up to the ~90 kHz the publisher can sustain, and it applies back-pressure instead of dropping.
  • SHM on is the best large-message option I measured: 4 MB at 1961 Hz.
  • Not helpful: transport/unicast/lowlatency=true made the tail worse (p99 6–8 ms).

C. Fast DDS: if you have to stay on it

export RMW_IMPLEMENTATION=rmw_fastrtps_cpp
export FASTDDS_BUILTIN_TRANSPORTS=UDPv4
# do NOT set ROS_AUTOMATIC_DISCOVERY_RANGE=LOCALHOST
  • Removes the 5.7 ms tick: 2 kHz at ~85 µs; clean to 20 kHz.
  • Gotcha: with ROS_AUTOMATIC_DISCOVERY_RANGE=LOCALHOST, rmw_fastrtps sets up its own SHM + UDP transports (rmw_fastrtps_shared_cpp/src/participant.cpp, Jazzy and Kilted). The variable is then ignored, and I measured the same ~330 Hz / 5 ms behaviour.
  • Tuning: a UDP-only XML profile with 4 MB socket buffers performed the same as the variable.
  • Avoid:
    • RMW_FASTRTPS_PUBLICATION_MODE=ASYNCHRONOUS: publish() threw cannot publish data at 5 kHz and aborted the publisher;
    • 4 MB messages: none arrived.

D. Cyclone DDS + iceoryx: fastest between processes (C++ only)

Both the Jazzy and Kilted kegs ship Cyclone built with iceoryx, plus iox-roudi and iox-introspection-client. Config files: ros2-macos-rmw-bench/config at main · idesign0/ros2-macos-rmw-bench · GitHub

  1. Start RouDi, the iceoryx daemon. Run one per machine, before or together with your nodes; nodes that start first wait for it.
    iox-roudi                                   # default pools: chunks up to 4 MB
iox-roudi -c roudi_config_large.toml        # adds 8 MB chunks, for messages >= ~4 MB
  2. Enable shared memory in Cyclone:
    export RMW_IMPLEMENTATION=rmw_cyclonedds_cpp
export CYCLONEDDS_URI='<CycloneDDS><Domain><SharedMemory><Enable>true</Enable></SharedMemory></Domain></CycloneDDS>'
    Or merge the <SharedMemory> block into the Cyclone XML you already use.
  3. In a launch file, add ExecuteProcess(cmd=['iox-roudi']) and set both environment variables with SetEnvironmentVariable. There’s a working example in the repo.
  4. Verify it’s used: latency is the clearest sign, ~10 µs instead of ~70–90 µs over UDP. iox-introspection-client --port lists the ports.

Results: 2 kHz at ~35 µs; 200 kHz at 10 / 27 µs; 1 MB messages at 1982 Hz, 100 µs.

Rules (from rmw_cyclonedds’ shared memory doc and my measurements):

  • Types: zero-copy only for fixed-size message types: no strings, no sequences, so nothing with a Header. Other types are serialized into shared memory, a smaller gain.
  • QoS: KEEP_LAST history; reliable and best effort both work. The doc says publisher depth ≤ 16; this build still used shared memory at 17 and 100, but keep ≤ 16 to be safe.
  • Limits: one RouDi per machine; at most 127 subscriptions per process.
  • Memory pool: if no chunk fits, publish() blocks forever. A 4 MB message with the default pools hung my publisher.
  • No RouDi: every node waits for it and aborts after ~60 s.

Bugs I hit (Kilted, Cyclone 0.10.5), reported in Issues · ros2/rmw_cyclonedds · GitHub

  • Duplicates between C++ and Python: when one endpoint is rclcpp and the other rclpy, every message is delivered twice, in both directions. C++ ↔ C++ and Python ↔ Python are fine; every C++ number above was checked for duplicates.
  • QoS check bypassed: a reliable subscriber receives data from a best-effort publisher over shared memory, even though ROS logs the QoS as incompatible.
  • Consequence: ros2 topic hz shows 2× the real rate.
  • Known upstream: transient_local over SHM (ros2/rmw_cyclonedds#401).

E. Composition: fastest with no daemon at all

Load publisher and subscriber as components into one component_container, with use_intra_process_comms: true. Messages never leave the process: 100 kHz at 3 / 19 µs on Cyclone. This works with any RMW.

F. For every option

  • Queue depth: with bursty delivery (Fast DDS SHM, USB-serial sensors), KEEP_LAST(1) / SensorDataQoS silently drops most messages. If every sample matters, use a deeper queue, for example rclcpp::QoS(50).best_effort().
  • USB-serial sensors: macOS has no equivalent of the Linux udev latency_timer=1 rule for FTDI adapters. Data arrives in ~16 ms batches regardless of RMW.
  • Executor: the rclcpp EventsExecutor was within 5–10 % of the default at 2 kHz, so it’s not worth switching for this.
  • Background load: Spotlight indexing or builds cause occasional 0.1–0.4 s stalls on every transport at high rates.
  • Build in Release when you benchmark. An unoptimized build of my own benchmark cut large-message throughput ~3.5×.

Method and caveats

  • Same machine, Release build. Each point is a 4 s window after 1.5 s of discovery; ranges are over repeated runs, measured on a quiet machine.
  • The rate sweep’s subscriber busy-spins; a normal rclcpp::spin() node will be a little lower.
  • Linux was not measured on the same hardware, so I can’t give a side-by-side comparison.
  • Reproduce: scripts/full_sweep.sh in GitHub - idesign0/ros2-macos-rmw-bench: How fast can ROS 2 nodes talk on macOS? Inter-process rate/latency benchmark for Fast DDS, Cyclone DDS (+iceoryx), Zenoh and composition, with reproducers for macOS-specific issues. · GitHub runs everything above; RESULTS.md has every number.

Feedback welcome, especially from anyone who sees different numbers on Intel Macs or other macOS versions.

Runtime governability in ROS 2: where should changing operating conditions be handled?
from ROS Discourse General

Hi all,

I’m looking for architectural experience around a situation in which a ROS 2 system remains technically healthy, but an external operating condition changes while the robot is already performing a task.

ROS 2 already provides important mechanisms for component lifecycle management, supervision and recovery. Nav2 adds planning, control, recovery behaviour and local collision-related mechanisms.

My question is adjacent to those capabilities, rather than a proposal to replace them.

Consider a mobile robot navigating in a shared facility.

Localization is valid. The planner and controller are operating normally. There is no immediate collision hazard and no component failure.

While the task is underway, however, an external operating condition changes. For example, an area that was previously available for the operation is temporarily no longer available.

Where should that kind of changing context be represented and evaluated in a real ROS 2 architecture?

Possible locations might include:

  • the task or mission layer;
  • a separate supervisory component;
  • a fleet or facility-level service;
  • or a combination of local and external components.

I would particularly value practical experience around four questions:

  1. Which layer should own and update this kind of operating context?

  2. How should the system behave if the external source of that context becomes unavailable or outdated?

  3. How can higher-level operational logic be introduced without weakening existing local safety, recovery or lifecycle behaviour?

  4. In a multi-robot environment, which parts of the operating context should belong to each robot and which should be shared?

I have read the recent discussions here around runtime accountability and runtime trust or authorization. My question is deliberately narrower: the technical stack is functioning as designed, but the conditions surrounding the operation have changed.

At Sentryn, we use “runtime governability” as a public research term for this broader class of questions. We are not announcing a ROS package or claiming a completed ROS 2 integration.

I am mainly interested in existing architectural patterns, operational experience, counterexamples and relevant prior work.

Public research-level context:

sentryn.com

Runtime Governance for Autonomous Systems | Sentryn

A conceptual overview of runtime governance for autonomous systems: how oversight and accountability remain relevant while autonomous operations are underway.

Critical feedback is very welcome, particularly from teams operating ROS 2 robots outside controlled laboratory environments.

What happened to "Robot-as-a-Snap"?
from ROS Discourse General

If you’re building on ROS 2, odds are you’re running Ubuntu. It’s the default for almost everyone. Over the last few years, Canonical has put a ton of engineering effort into pushing an Ubuntu Robotics ecosystem built around Snaps and Ubuntu Core.

On paper, it sounds like a dream setup for production robots:

  • Atomic & Transactional Updates: Ubuntu Core gives you atomic OS updates and transactional Snap updates. If an update breaks midway through, it automatically rolls back instead of bricking a robot in the field.

  • Version Linking: You can link snap dependencies across versions so you know everything is compatible before triggering an update across the fleet.

  • Built-in ROS 2 Tooling: Snapcraft has ROS 2 extensions and build plugins ready to go out of the box.

  • Observability & Fleet Management: Hooks into the Canonical Observability Stack (Telegraf, Prometheus, Loki) for real-time telemetry, plus Landscape to manage snap versions across your fleet.

Yet, despite all this tech being around for years, there’s surprisingly little chatter about it in the ROS 2 community. On Discourse and in forums, I rarely see people asking questions, sharing setups, or recommending snaps for production.

Instead, when people talk about shipping robots, it usually comes down to:

  1. Standard apt on Ubuntu Server: Easy to set up, but updates are fragile. A failed apt upgrade can easily leave a robot in a broken state out in the field.

  2. Docker: Great for bundling dependencies, but configuring hardware passthrough for heavy GPU workloads like VSLAM or LLMs can add friction.

  3. Yocto / Custom Embedded Linux: Gives you total control and clean atomic updates, but now your team is stuck maintaining every single system package.

Where’s the catch?

Why hasn’t the snap ecosystem become the standard way to reliably deploy software onto robot fleets?

  • Is strict confinement getting in the way of hardware access (like passing through GPUs for VSLAM/LLMs, direct serial, GPIO, or cameras)?

  • Is the developer iteration loop just too clunky compared to plain workspace builds?

  • Or are people just happier sticking with Docker or Yocto despite the maintenance headaches?

Curious to hear from anyone deploying ROS 2 in the wild: What are you actually running on your fleet, and what kept you on (or off) Snaps and Ubuntu Core?

A scenario generator. It writes forest scenarios for a quadrotor: a patrol route, the width of the lane cleared along it, poles placed near the route, tree density, wind, cruise speed and altitude. Each submission writes 20 scenarios on an undisclosed seed; every scenario is flown three times. How much RTK fix do your outdoor robots keep under tree canopy? Field data and a test design
from ROS Discourse General

For those of you running outdoor robots (lawn mowers, orchard or vineyard platforms, campus AMRs), how much RTK fix do you actually keep under tree canopy?

We recently went through a field test that logged several RTK receivers side by side under roadside trees, and I thought the numbers, and their limits, might be useful here.

What was logged (all at about 10 Hz, viewed in RTKPLOT):

  • Half sky + rain, 21.9 km baseline, about 61 min
  • Under trees + rain, 16.4 km baseline, about 39 min
  • Under trees + network RTK, 1.99 km baseline, about 61 min

What the status bars showed:

  • In both long-baseline sessions, three of four record groups (Septentrio mosaic-X5 / mosaic-H based receivers) held 100% fixed (Q=1) for the whole session.
  • One record group dropped to 83.3% (half sky) and 88.5% (under trees) fixed, with the rest float or DGPS. The ground track shows jumps of tens of centimetres when that happens.
  • On the 1.99 km network RTK session, both record groups held 100%.

What it can’t tell you: there was no ground truth and no raw observation data, the sessions changed canopy, baseline, correction source and weather all at once, and there was one unit per position. So it’s a status comparison, not an accuracy ranking. A fixed flag doesn’t prove the position was right.

If you’re evaluating receivers for a robot that works under trees, this is the test design we’d recommend:

  1. Same site, baseline and correction source; cross open-sky/under-canopy with dry/wet, three or more repeats each
  2. A known control point or a long static baseline as truth
  3. Log raw observations so every receiver can be reprocessed with the same RTKLIB settings
  4. At least two units per model; log heading at the same time on dual-antenna setups
  5. Report RMS and 95th percentile, time to first fix and availability, not only fix ratio, after decimating to 1 Hz

On the ROS side, the septentrio_gnss_driver publishes the solution status, so you can log fix state alongside your localization output and see exactly where under the canopy your EKF starts leaning on odometry.

The full write-up with all 36 figures and per-screenshot readings is here: RTK fix ratio under tree canopy and rain

I’d be interested to hear what others see in practice: do you gate your localization on fix status, or blend float solutions with a larger covariance?

Disclosure: I work with Eview GNSS, which makes receivers based on Septentrio modules. Test data was supplied by the test team and is used with permission.
